# Argument-map example

This compact example shows how `planning-humanities-argument` turns a promising claim into a traceable claim–reason–evidence structure.

## Input

> Digital interfaces do not merely represent archives; they reorganize what users can notice, connect, and remember.

## Output

| ID | Claim | Reason | Evidence required | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| C1 | Interface design participates in archival interpretation. | Search, ranking, and navigation make some relations easier to perceive than others. | Interface walkthroughs, design documentation, comparative user paths. | “Participates” may be confused with total determination. |
| C2 | This participation has epistemic consequences. | Repeated pathways stabilize some associations and obscure alternatives. | Close analysis of defaults and comparative retrieval outcomes. | Requires evidence beyond a single interface. |
| C3 | The effect is historically specific rather than universal. | Different interface regimes organize access differently. | Comparison across at least two archival systems or versions. | Avoid a linear technological narrative. |

## Dependency check

- C2 depends on C1.
- C3 limits the scope of C1 and C2.
- No claim is publication-ready until the evidence column is populated with verified materials.

## Why this example matters

The skill does not reward a smooth paragraph. It reveals what must be demonstrated and where the argument can fail.
