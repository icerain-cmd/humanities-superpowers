# Reviewing a Manuscript

## Purpose

Review a manuscript as an integrated scholarly argument rather than as a collection of polished paragraphs. The review identifies strengths, blocking weaknesses, revision priorities, and decisions that remain the researcher's responsibility.

This skill is not a simulated hostile reviewer and not a guarantee of acceptance. Its task is to reconstruct the manuscript fairly, test whether its parts support one another, and produce an actionable revision map.

## Contract

**Accepts** a complete or substantial manuscript, research brief, argument map, journal guidelines, prior gate reports, citation audit, terminology ledger, or reviewer criteria.

**Requires** enough of the manuscript to reconstruct its central question, thesis, evidence, method, and contribution. A target genre or venue is strongly recommended.

**Produces** a manuscript review, reconstructed argument, strengths, findings by severity, revision priorities, venue-fit observations where evidence is available, and a manuscript-review gate report.

**May produce** section-level annotations, a revision sequence, an editorial triage table, or handoffs to citation, terminology, scope, argument, or peer-review-response skills.

**Fails when** the manuscript is unavailable, only a title or abstract is supplied for whole-manuscript judgment, the requested review requires invented sources or reviewer opinions, or venue criteria cannot be verified.

**Guarantees** that major criticisms are linked to observable manuscript evidence and that severity is distinguished from stylistic preference.

**Does not guarantee** publication, reviewer agreement, originality, factual truth, or disciplinary adequacy beyond the materials and expertise available.

## When to use

Use after a coherent draft exists, before external circulation, after major structural revision, before selecting final submission status, or when a researcher needs a prioritized diagnosis rather than line editing.

Do not use this skill as a substitute for citation audit, specialist fact checking, or copyediting. It may identify the need for those operations and route the manuscript accordingly.

## Inputs required

Provide:

1. The manuscript, including notes and bibliography where possible.
2. The intended article, chapter, conference, or book genre.
3. Target venue guidelines or evaluation criteria, if fit is requested.
4. The researcher's stated question, thesis, and claimed contribution.
5. Available argument maps, audit reports, or reviewer history.
6. Any sections that must not be rewritten without approval.

If the stated thesis differs from what the manuscript actually argues, preserve both and report the discrepancy.

## Procedure

### 1. Establish review scope

State what was received, what was not received, which criteria will be used, and whether citation content, factual claims, language quality, and venue fit are included or only flagged for separate review.

### 2. Reconstruct the manuscript charitably

Before criticizing, summarize:

- research problem;
- central question;
- thesis;
- method or interpretive practice;
- corpus or evidence;
- chapter or section sequence;
- claimed contribution.

Use the manuscript's strongest defensible formulation. If reconstruction requires guessing, mark the ambiguity.

### 3. Test question–thesis alignment

Determine whether the thesis answers the research question and whether the conclusion answers the same question introduced at the beginning. Flag a moving research object, broadened conclusion, or question that is descriptive while the thesis is causal or normative.

### 4. Test scope and evidence coverage

Compare the manuscript's claims with its corpus, historical range, linguistic range, and method. Identify where local readings become field-wide, historical, technological, social, or universal claims without sufficient bridge evidence.

### 5. Test concept architecture

Check whether central concepts are defined, distinguished from adjacent terms, used at a stable level of analysis, and connected to their sources or lineage. A theoretically impressive vocabulary is not a contribution if it does no identifiable analytical work.

### 6. Test argument structure

Reconstruct major claims, evidence, warrants, objections, and consequences. Identify missing warrants, circular reasoning, unsupported transitions, redundant sections, late definitions, and conclusions introduced before evidence.

Ask whether each section changes what the reader can reasonably accept. Sections that only repeat context or theorist summaries may need compression or relocation.

### 7. Test close readings and examples

Assess whether observed features are distinguished from interpretation, whether alternative readings are addressed, and whether examples support the scale of the claim. Do not judge an interpretation solely because another reading is possible; judge whether the manuscript makes its interpretive path accountable.

### 8. Test literature positioning and contribution

Determine whether prior work is represented as a structured scholarly conversation rather than a list. Evaluate whether the contribution is stated as a bounded intervention: new object, relation, distinction, reinterpretation, method, synthesis, or consequence.

Do not accept “no one has studied this” without documented review scope.

### 9. Inspect citation and terminology status

Use existing audit reports if provided. Otherwise flag risks without claiming full verification. Identify central claims whose support is unclear, direct quotations needing audit, inconsistent key terms, and translations requiring specialist confirmation.

### 10. Classify findings by severity

Use:

- **Fatal**: the central thesis cannot currently be sustained.
- **Major**: substantial new evidence, reframing, or reorganization is required.
- **Moderate**: the core can stand, but an important section or distinction needs revision.
- **Minor**: localized clarity, style, or formatting issue.

Severity MUST reflect argumentative consequence, not reviewer irritation.

### 11. Prioritize revision

Organize recommendations in dependency order. Do not begin with prose polishing when the research question, evidence, or argument structure may change. A typical sequence is:

```text
question/scope -> concept -> evidence -> argument -> objections -> citations -> terminology -> prose/format
```

For each major issue state the observed evidence, why it matters, the smallest adequate remedy, and which skill should handle it.

### 12. Issue the gate report

Return `PASS` only when no blocking issue remains within the declared review scope. Use `CONDITIONAL PASS` when the argument is viable but external audit, specialist review, or localized revision remains. Return `FAIL` when central evidence, scope, concept, or logic does not support the thesis.

## Stop signals

Stop or narrow the review when:

- only an abstract is available for a whole-manuscript judgment;
- source verification is requested without sources;
- journal instructions cannot be verified;
- the review would require specialist linguistic, archival, legal, or statistical competence not available;
- the requester asks for praise, hostility, or a predetermined accept/reject verdict;
- anonymous reviewer identities or motives are requested without evidence.

## Completion criteria

A review is complete when:

- the manuscript is fairly reconstructed;
- every major criticism cites an observable feature or location;
- question, scope, concepts, evidence, argument, interpretation, contribution, citations, and terminology have been considered;
- findings are severity-ranked;
- revision actions are dependency-ordered;
- external-review needs are explicit;
- the gate status follows from the findings.

## Anti-fabrication rules

Do not invent reviewer consensus, journal preferences, source contents, missing sections, disciplinary trends, or likely acceptance rates. Do not claim a manuscript is original because no duplicate wording was noticed. Do not attribute motives to the author. Do not manufacture criticisms merely to appear rigorous.

When evidence is unavailable, write “not assessed” or route to the appropriate audit.

## Output format

Provide:

1. Review scope and limitations.
2. Charitable reconstruction.
3. Principal strengths.
4. Findings by severity, each with evidence and consequence.
5. Revision priorities in dependency order.
6. Required specialist or external checks.
7. Recommended next skills.

### Gate report

```text
Gate: Manuscript Review
Status: PASS | CONDITIONAL PASS | FAIL
Evidence checked:
Blocking issues:
Non-blocking risks:
Researcher decisions required:
Required next action:
Next skill:
```

## Good invocation

> Review this article as an integrated humanities argument. Reconstruct it fairly, rank problems by severity, cite section locations, and do not claim that citations are verified unless an audit report supports that claim.

## Bad invocation

> Pretend to be an angry Reviewer 2, invent devastating objections, and tell me the journal will reject it.

The bad invocation requests theater and unsupported prediction rather than evidence-based review.

## Limitations

A manuscript review is conditional on disciplinary context, source access, and the declared criteria. It cannot replace expert peer review, editor judgment, language editing, legal review, ethics review, or empirical validation. Different qualified reviewers may disagree; the skill makes reasons and dependencies explicit rather than claiming a single final verdict.
